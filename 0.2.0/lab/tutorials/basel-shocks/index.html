    Tutorial: the Basel shocks¶

    Time: 20 minutes · You need: the browser app · You will learn: what the six Basel interest-rate shocks are, how to run them, and how to compare banks under the same shock.

    The six shocks¶

    The Basel Committee's standard for interest-rate risk in the banking book (IRRBB, SRP31) defines six shocks to the yield curve. For US dollars:

    Shock What moves Size
    Parallel up every maturity +200bp
    Parallel down every maturity −200bp
    Short rates up short maturities, fading with maturity +300bp at the short end
    Short rates down the same, downwards −300bp at the short end
    Steepener short rates down, long rates up −195bp short, +135bp long
    Flattener short rates up, long rates down +240bp short, −90bp long

    The short-rate shocks decay with maturity t in years as e−t/4. The steepener and flattener combine the short and long shapes. Supervisors use these shocks to compare banks: a bank whose economic value falls by more than 15% of Tier 1 capital in the worst of the six is an outlier that draws attention.

    In the lab a shock is applied on the start date to the Treasury curve, and the curve then stays at that shocked level for the whole horizon. You can also phase a shock in over a number of days.

    Part 1: all six shocks on one bank¶

    1. Open Banks → Silicon Valley Bank, end-2021.
    2. Leave the horizon at 12 months and click Run all Basel shocks.

    The lab starts seven runs (historical rates plus the six shocks) and opens Compare when they finish. The summary table is sorted by each run's lowest CET1 ratio. Look at:

    • HTM unrealised: the parallel-up shock costs SVB −$10.3bn on HTM securities, and the actual path of 2022 cost −$12.7bn. The real 2022 was worse than the regulatory +200bp shock, because the 10-year yield rose about 240bp.
    • Worst ΔEVE/T1: SVB's economic value falls by more than half of its Tier 1 capital in the worst shock. That makes it a clear outlier against the 15% threshold.

    Part 2: four banks under the same shock¶

    Now compare four banks under Parallel up (+200bp), with unrealised AFS losses counted in capital, as Basel III requires.

    For each of Silicon Valley Bank, end-2021, Signature Bank, end-2021, First Republic Bank, end-2021 and JPMorgan Chase Bank, end-2021:

    1. Open the bank.
    2. In Run a scenario, choose Basel IRRBB shocks → Parallel up (+200bp), horizon 12, AOCI filter: Off.
    3. Click Run scenario.

    Then open Runs, tick the four runs and click Compare 4 selected. Each run is labelled with its bank. Because the banks differ in size, the comparison shows unrealised gains and losses as a share of each bank's equity at the start: SVB's are almost twice its equity, at about −180%.

    Bank Lowest CET1 ratio Worst ΔEVE / Tier 1 HTM unrealised AFS unrealised
    Signature Bank 7.4% −20% −$0.4bn −$1.5bn
    First Republic Bank 11.0% −42% −$3.4bn −$0.7bn
    Silicon Valley Bank 12.2% −55% −$10.3bn −$2.8bn
    JPMorgan Chase Bank 13.6% −10% −$28.4bn −$23.3bn

    How to read it:

    • Capital. With AOCI counted, the shock hits CET1 on day one through AFS losses. Signature starts with the thinnest capital and drops to 7.4%. All four then recover as profits accumulate.
    • Economic value. ΔEVE revalues every asset and liability at the shocked rates, including the stable (core) part of deposits. Three of the four banks are outliers against the 15% threshold; JPMorgan is not. SVB is the most exposed: long securities, funded by deposits that were 95% uninsured and so count as less stable. First Republic's exposure is in long fixed-rate mortgages, which are loans rather than securities: they show up in EVE and in loan unrealised losses, not in HTM or AFS.
    • Size. JPMorgan's dollar losses are the largest, but against its capital its economic-value loss is the smallest, and its larger share of insured retail deposits offsets more of it. Its $46bn of goodwill and other intangibles is deducted from CET1, as First Republic's and Signature's preferred stock is (it counts as Additional Tier 1, not common equity).

    Deposits in ΔEVE

    Core deposits follow the Basel caps, weighted by each bank's insured share (assumptions). Untick Core deposits when importing a bank to see the exposure of the assets alone.

    Part 3: phase a shock in¶

    A shock that arrives over a year is gentler than one that arrives overnight, because the bank keeps earning while it builds. On SVB, run Parallel up (+200bp) with Phase in over (days) set to 365, and compare it with the instant shock. The unrealised losses end in the same place; the CET1 path is smoother.

    Part 4: your own curve shift¶

    Choose Key-rate shift… and enter a shift in basis points at 3M, 1Y, 2Y, 5Y, 10Y and 30Y. Between those maturities the shift is interpolated linearly; beyond them it is flat. For example, a bear flattener: 3M +300, 1Y +250, 2Y +200, 5Y +100, 10Y +50, 30Y +25.

    What you have learned¶

    • The six Basel shocks give a common yardstick across banks.
    • Capital measures (with or without AOCI) and economic value tell different stories: one is the accounting view, the other the market view of the whole balance sheet.
    • A shock's speed matters for capital, not for the final unrealised loss.
